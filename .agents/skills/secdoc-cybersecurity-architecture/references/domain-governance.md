# Business & Governance Architecture

## Scope

This domain covers the security program itself as an architecture problem: operating model and org design, the policy hierarchy, risk management mechanics, metrics and reporting, third-party risk, budget, and the compliance program as an integration layer. Boundary with neighbors: risk-assessment and RMF methodology cite `nist-sp.md` (800-30/800-37) — this file covers the *machinery* around them; regime specifics live in `compliance.md`; board and leadership deliverables compose with the executive-reporting skill — this file supplies what to report, that skill shapes the artifact.

## Architecture patterns

**The security program as architecture.** The program has an architecture the same way a system does: components (functions), interfaces (reporting lines, escalation paths, decision rights), and failure modes (gaps and overlaps in ownership). Design it deliberately:
- **Operating model** — which functions exist (architecture, engineering, operations/SOC, GRC, product security, IR), which are centralized versus federated into business units, and where the seams are. The federated question is the real one at scale: central policy with embedded security partners in the business beats both extremes (an ivory tower nobody consults; feral security teams diverging per BU) — but only if the embedded roles have a dotted line that means something.
- **Where the CISO reports, honestly optioned** — each answer costs something: **CIO** — natural IT alignment and budget adjacency, at the cost of grading your own boss's homework (security findings about IT land on the person who owns IT); **CEO/COO** — independence and business standing, at the cost of a board-adjacent seat the org may not staff or fund seriously; **CFO** — risk-and-audit affinity, at the cost of security framed as cost center; **General Counsel/CRO** — privilege and risk framing, at the cost of distance from engineering reality. There is no clean answer; name the conflict the chosen structure creates and the compensating mechanism (independent reporting channel to the audit committee is the usual one). Regulatory expectations increasingly force the question — verify current requirements for the sectors in play (e.g., SEC cyber disclosure rules' governance-description requirements, NYDFS 500's CISO provisions).

**Policy hierarchy — with drafting discipline.** Four layers, distinct on purpose: **Policy** (what and why — board/executive-approved, stable over years, mandates and assigns accountability); **Standard** (the measurable what — specific, testable requirements: "phishing-resistant MFA for privileged access"); **Procedure** (the how — stepwise, owned by the operating team); **Guideline** (recommended, explicitly non-mandatory). The drafting discipline that keeps the hierarchy honest: policies never name products or versions (that's why they survive re-platforming); standards are written testably (an auditor can verify compliance without interviewing the author); every mandatory statement has an owner, an enforcement mechanism, and an exception process with expiry — **an exception process is not optional**: without one, exceptions happen anyway, undocumented, and the policy quietly becomes fiction. Review cycles per layer (policies ~annually or on major change; standards more often — they carry the version-sensitive content). A policy set nobody can map to its enforcement mechanisms is shelfware; assess the mapping, not the prose.

**Risk management architecture.** Methodology lives in `nist-sp.md` (SP 800-30 assessment chain, 800-37 RMF lifecycle); the machinery around it:
- **Risk register mechanics** — entries traceable through the 800-30 chain, each with an *owner in the business* (the risk belongs to whoever owns the process/asset, not to security — security maintains the register, the business owns the risks), review dates, and current treatment status. A register that only security reads is a diary, not a governance instrument.
- **Acceptance mechanics** — risk acceptance is a signed decision by someone with authority commensurate to the exposure, time-boxed with re-review, not an email thread that trails off. Escalation thresholds defined in advance (what magnitude requires VP/CxO/board sign-off) so acceptance authority isn't negotiated per incident.
- **Quantification, honestly assessed** — **FAIR** at orientation depth: decomposes risk into loss-event frequency and loss magnitude, forcing explicit, calibratable estimates — genuinely useful for comparing options and sizing the top risks in money, and honest about its costs: estimate-hungry, analyst-intensive, and precision-theater-prone when inputs are guesses dressed as data (a distribution built on made-up numbers is a made-up distribution with error bars). The defensible middle: qualitative registers for breadth, quantified deep-dives for the top handful of decisions where the investment case needs numbers. Cyber-insurance interaction: quantification increasingly feeds coverage sizing, and underwriting questionnaires have become a de facto control mandate — see budget, below.

**Metrics and reporting architecture.** The design test for every metric: *what decision changes based on this number?* KRIs (leading exposure indicators — unpatched-critical dwell time on internet-facing systems, phishing-resistant-MFA coverage of privileged accounts, orphaned-account count, mean time to revoke leaver access) versus KPIs (program performance — detection coverage of the relevant technique shortlist, time-to-triage, control-test pass rates). The vanity-metric trap named: blocked-attack counts, raw alert volumes, training completion percentages — numbers that only go up and change no decisions. Each metric needs a target, a threshold that triggers action, and an owner; a dashboard without thresholds is decoration. **Board reporting**: business-risk language, trend over snapshot, decisions-needed over accomplishments — peer comparison and scenario framing ("what would this loss event cost") beat control inventories; compose with the executive-reporting skill for the deliverable itself; this file supplies the content architecture (which risks, which KRIs, what decision the board is being asked to make).

**Third-party risk architecture.** Tiering is the architecture — assessment depth must follow risk, not vendor count:
- **Tier drivers**: data access (classification level touched — a GDPR processor relationship is a tier driver by itself, Art. 28 — `compliance.md`), system access (network/API connectivity, standing credentials — `domain-iam.md` NHI discipline applies to vendor accounts), and criticality (would their outage stop us — concentration risk included).
- **Assessment depth by tier**: top tier — evidence-based (SOC 2 Type II *read, not filed* — scope, exceptions, CUECs actually reviewed; pen-test summaries; contractual security schedules with audit rights and breach-notification clocks that fit your own obligations, e.g., GDPR's 72 hours upstream); mid tier — standardized questionnaire plus external attack-surface signal; low tier — contractual baseline and inventory presence. Questionnaires are honesty-optional; weight evidence over attestation at the top tier.
- **Continuous monitoring honesty** — security-rating services measure external hygiene, which correlates loosely with internal control quality: useful as a *change* detector (rating drops → ask why) and a screening signal, misleading as an assurance substitute. The real continuous controls: contract clauses that oblige disclosure, offboarding that actually revokes access (vendor accounts and NHIs die when the contract does), and fourth-party awareness for the top tier (your critical vendor's critical vendors — the sub-processor chain, in GDPR terms).

**Security budget architecture.** Structure the argument, not just the number: baseline run cost (the control estate's operating spend — licensing, staffing, managed services) separated from change/uplift investment (new capability tied to named risks), so cuts and asks are legible. **Control-value arguments** beat fear arguments: tie each material investment to the risk it reduces (register-linked), the techniques it addresses (`mitre-attack.md` shortlist), or the obligation it satisfies (`compliance.md` — regime-mandated spend is the easiest budget line to defend and should be labeled as such). Benchmarks (security as % of IT spend) are context, not justification — they prove nothing about *your* risk. **Insurance-driven requirements** are now a budget force: underwriters mandate specific controls (MFA everywhere, EDR coverage, tested backups, PAM) as coverage conditions — sometimes aligned with your risk priorities, sometimes not; cost them explicitly as insurance-enablement rather than letting them silently reorder the roadmap, and weigh premium reduction against control cost like any other investment.

**Compliance program architecture — the integration point.** Run per-regime, compliance is N parallel programs each collecting its own evidence, interpreting its own controls, and exhausting the same engineers with overlapping questionnaires. The architecture that fixes it: **one control set, mapped outward** — a unified control framework (org-defined, commonly scaffolded on 800-53 or ISO 27001 Annex A) where each control carries mappings to every regime that claims it (the crosswalk pattern and table in `compliance.md`: one access-review process satisfying SOX ITGC, SOC 2 CC6.2/CC6.3, PCI Req. 7, GDPR Art. 25/32 access limitation, and 800-171 3.1 simultaneously). **Test once, satisfy many**: a shared evidence repository with control-to-evidence mapping, evidence generated by the controls themselves where possible (the SOX lesson in `compliance.md` generalized), and one assessment calendar so audits draw from the same well instead of re-interviewing the same people quarterly. The per-regime deltas (PCI's prescriptive parameters, CMMC's binary-per-requirement scoring, SOC 2's period-of-time evidence) are handled as documented deltas on the common control, not as forked control sets. This is where governance earns its budget line: every regime added to a mapped framework costs the delta; every regime run standalone costs the whole thing again.

## Control mapping

- **SP 800-53 Rev. 5**: PM family (program management — PM-1 policy, PM-9 risk management strategy, PM-28 risk framing, PM-3 resources, PM-6 measures of performance, PM-30 supply chain risk strategy), PL-1/PL-2 (planning), RA-1/RA-3 (risk assessment process — with `nist-sp.md`), CA-2/CA-5 (assessments and POA&Ms), SR-6 (supplier assessments and reviews).
- **CSF 2.0**: the **Govern** function is this domain's home — GV.OC (organizational context), GV.RM (risk management strategy), GV.RR (roles and responsibilities), GV.PO (policy), GV.OV (oversight), GV.SC (supply chain) — the 2.0 addition that elevated governance to a function, and the right spine for assessing a program's shape.
- **Compliance hooks**: every regime lands here as program obligation — SOX's ICFR governance and ITGC ownership, SOC 2 CC1–CC5 (the governance criteria: control environment, communication, risk assessment, monitoring), PCI Req. 12 (policy and program), GDPR accountability (Art. 5(2)), Art. 24 (controller responsibility), Art. 30 (records of processing), DPO provisions (Art. 37–39), CMMC's SSP/POA&M/affirmation machinery and SPRS posture — see `compliance.md` throughout.

## Decision criteria

- **Small org**: no standalone GRC function — a lean policy/standard set (own what you can enforce), a top-10 risk register the leadership team actually reviews, compliance scoped to what contracts demand, security leadership as a strong fractional/deputized role with a direct line to the CEO.
- **Mid-size**: first dedicated GRC hire builds the unified control framework *before* the second regime lands (the integration architecture is cheap early, expensive late); risk acceptance mechanics formalized; vendor tiering stood up with the top tier evidence-based; metrics with thresholds replacing status decks.
- **Large / regulated**: federated operating model with real dotted lines, quantified risk deep-dives for the top exposures, compliance-as-code evidence automation where control telemetry supports it, board reporting on a fixed KRI set with trend history, and the CISO-reporting conflict named and compensated in writing.
- Universal ordering: ownership and decision rights → policy hierarchy with exceptions machinery → risk register the business owns → unified control framework → then quantification and automation. A GRC platform purchased before the control framework exists automates the absence of one.

## Assessment questions

- Who owns each of the top ten risks — name the business owner, the last review date, and the treatment decision on record. If security owns them all, that's the finding.
- Show me a risk acceptance from the last year: who signed, at what exposure level, with what expiry — and has it been re-reviewed?
- Pick a standard (say, MFA): where's the enforcement mechanism, how many exceptions exist, and do the exceptions have owners and expiry dates?
- Which metrics went to leadership last quarter, and what decision did any of them change? Which metric would trigger action tomorrow if it crossed its threshold — and what's the threshold?
- How many compliance regimes apply, and how many control sets exist — one mapped framework or N parallel ones? Show the same control (access reviews) satisfying three regimes with one evidence set.
- For the top-tier vendor with the deepest data access: who read their last SOC 2 report, what exceptions did it contain, and what happens to their accounts and credentials when the contract ends?
- What did the cyber-insurance questionnaire mandate, what did those controls cost, and were they on the roadmap for risk reasons or coverage reasons?
- Where does the CISO report, what conflict does that create, and what compensates for it?
