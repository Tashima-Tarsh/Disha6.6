# Compliance Regimes — SOX, SOC 2, PCI DSS, GDPR, CMMC

Five regimes that answer different questions to different audiences. Getting the framing right matters more than memorizing requirements: **SOX** protects the integrity of financial reporting (audience: external financial auditor, SEC); **SOC 2** demonstrates a service organization's controls to customers (audience: customers' auditors, via a CPA's attestation); **PCI DSS** protects cardholder data (audience: acquiring banks and card brands, via QSA/SAQ); **GDPR** protects the rights of individuals whose personal data is processed (audience: EU/EEA supervisory authorities and data subjects, enforced by fines and orders); **CMMC** verifies that defense contractors protect FCI and CUI (audience: the DoD, via self-assessment or C3PAO certification). An architecture serving several of these should be one control set with per-regime mapping overlays — see the crosswalk section.

## SOX (Sarbanes-Oxley) — the IT angle

Security architects touch SOX through **ITGCs** (IT General Controls) supporting **ICFR** (internal control over financial reporting) under §302/§404. Scope is systems that feed financial reporting — the ERP, financial close tools, and everything in their dependency chain (interfaces, databases, job schedulers, the IdP that gates access to them). Scoping is where architecture engages: a clean segmentation between financial and non-financial systems shrinks the audit perimeter.

ITGC domains auditors test, every year:
- **Access to programs and data**: provisioning/deprovisioning tied to HR events, periodic access reviews with evidence of action taken, privileged access management, SoD (segregation of duties — both within the app's transaction flows and between dev and production access).
- **Change management**: authorized, tested, approved changes; developers cannot push to production unilaterally; emergency-change process with retrospective approval.
- **Computer operations**: job monitoring, backup/restore evidence, incident handling for in-scope systems.

Most SOX IT findings are process-evidence failures, not technical failures: the review happened but nobody recorded remediation of flagged accounts; the change was approved but after deployment. Architect for *evidence generation* — controls that produce their own audit trail beat controls that require manual attestation. There is no prescriptive SOX control catalog; audit firms test against COSO-informed ITGC expectations, so recommendations should reference the ITGC domain, not a fictional "SOX control number."

## SOC 2

Governed by the AICPA **Trust Services Criteria** (2017 TSC, with points of focus; verify current edition). **Security (Common Criteria CC1–CC9) is mandatory**; Availability, Confidentiality, Processing Integrity, and Privacy are opt-in categories the organization selects. Cite criteria by ID: CC6.1 (logical access), CC6.6 (external access/boundary protection), CC7.2 (monitoring for anomalies), CC8.1 (change management), etc.

- **Type I** = design of controls at a point in time; **Type II** = design *and operating effectiveness* over a period (typically 6–12 months). Customers overwhelmingly want Type II; advise accordingly.
- SOC 2 is criteria-based, not control-prescriptive: the org defines its controls and the auditor tests whether they meet the criteria. Architecture's job is choosing controls that are both effective and *cheaply evidenceable* over the observation window — a control that requires quarterly manual screenshot-gathering will fail eventually through fatigue.
- The **system description** defines audit scope. Keep the described system boundary honest and aligned with actual data flows; scope games surface in bridge letters and customer questionnaires.
- Common gap themes: vendor/subservice-organization management (carve-out vs. inclusive method), risk assessment as a living process rather than an annual document, and incident-response evidence.

## PCI DSS

Governed by the PCI Security Standards Council (https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org). Current major version **4.x** (v4.0.1 superseded v4.0; the future-dated v4 requirements became mandatory March 31, 2025 — verify current version and any new future-dated requirements before advising). Twelve requirements in six goals; cite at requirement-number granularity (e.g., 8.3.6 password length, 6.4.3 payment-page script management, 11.6.1 change detection on payment pages, 12.10.7 incident response for unexpected PAN discovery).

**Scoping is the whole game.** The CDE (cardholder data environment) is anything that stores, processes, or transmits cardholder data, *plus connected-to and security-impacting systems*. The architect's highest-value moves, in order: (1) eliminate cardholder data (redirect/iframe to a compliant PSP, tokenization) — data you don't have needs no protection; (2) segment aggressively and validate segmentation (required testing); (3) only then harden what remains in scope. Recommend the SAQ level honestly: an e-commerce redirect (SAQ A) is a different world from processing PAN server-side (SAQ D / ROC).

v4.x-specific themes to check in any current engagement: the **customized approach** option (objective-based alternative to defined requirements — requires documented targeted risk analysis), targeted risk analyses for frequency-based requirements, expanded MFA (all access into the CDE), and client-side/payment-page script controls (6.4.3, 11.6.1) driven by Magecart-class attacks.

## GDPR — the security architect's slice

Regulation (EU) 2016/679; canonical consolidated text via EUR-Lex (https://eur-lex.europa.eu). Cite at article level the way this file cites PCI DSS at requirement level: "Art. 32(1)(a) names pseudonymisation and encryption" is usable; "GDPR requires security" is not. GDPR is a data-protection law, not a security standard — most of it governs lawfulness, rights, and accountability — but a defined slice of it is architecture, and that slice is what this section covers.

**Art. 5 principles as architecture drivers.** The seven principles — lawfulness/fairness/transparency, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity and confidentiality, accountability (Art. 5(2)) — read to a lawyer as legal duties and to an architect as design constraints. Purpose limitation and minimisation are data-flow and retention architecture: collect less, keep it shorter, and be able to show why each field exists. Storage limitation forces retention schedules and defensible deletion into the design rather than the policy binder. Integrity and confidentiality (Art. 5(1)(f)) is the hook the security program hangs from. Accountability means every one of these must be *demonstrable* — evidence-generating controls again, same lesson as SOX.

**Lawful bases (Art. 6), at orientation depth.** Six bases: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, legitimate interests. The architect's involvement is narrow but real: the chosen basis shapes the system (consent requires capture, granularity, and withdrawal mechanics that propagate to processors; legitimate interests requires a documented balancing test). Which basis applies is a legal determination — flag it to counsel, don't resolve it.

**The two articles an architect designs against:**
- **Art. 25 — data protection by design and by default.** Design-time obligation: technical and organisational measures implementing the principles, built in "at the time of the determination of the means for processing," not retrofitted. By *default* means the default configuration processes only the personal data necessary for the specific purpose — default-minimal collection, default-shortest retention, default-narrowest access. In review terms: any system where privacy protection is an opt-in setting or a post-launch backlog item has an Art. 25 finding.
- **Art. 32 — security of processing.** The named measures: **(a)** pseudonymisation and encryption of personal data; **(b)** ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems; **(c)** the ability to restore availability and access after an incident (backup/recovery is a GDPR obligation, not just an ops nicety); **(d)** a process for regularly testing, assessing, and evaluating effectiveness. The standard is "appropriate to the risk" given state of the art and cost — risk-proportionate, not prescriptive. Map Art. 32 onto the existing control set (800-53 SC/CP/CA families, ISO 27001 Annex A) rather than building a parallel one.

**Breach notification as IR architecture (Art. 33/34).** Personal-data breaches go to the supervisory authority "without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours" after the controller becomes *aware* (Art. 33(1)), and to affected data subjects when the breach is likely to result in high risk to them (Art. 34). The 72-hour clock is an incident-response architecture requirement: detection and triage must be able to determine *whether personal data is involved, whose, and how much* inside that window, which means data classification and asset-to-data mapping feed IR, and processor contracts must obligate prompt notification upstream (Art. 33(2)). Art. 33(5) requires documenting all breaches, including ones not notified — the breach register is an evidence artifact to design for.

**Controller/processor roles and Art. 28 as vendor-architecture inputs.** The **controller** determines purposes and means; the **processor** processes on the controller's behalf. Art. 28 requires a contract (the DPA) binding the processor to documented instructions, confidentiality, Art. 32 security, sub-processor controls, breach notification, deletion/return at end of engagement, and audit rights. For the architect this is third-party risk with teeth: the vendor tiering model (see `domain-governance.md`) should treat "processes personal data as our processor" as a tier-driving attribute, and sub-processor chains are part of the data-flow diagram, not a legal footnote.

**DPIAs (Art. 35) as the assessment artifact.** Required where processing is "likely to result in a high risk" to individuals — systematic large-scale profiling, large-scale special-category data, systematic public monitoring, and whatever the relevant supervisory authority's published DPIA-required list adds. Methodologically a DPIA is a risk assessment with the risk borne by the *data subject*, not the organization — the SP 800-30 chain applies but the impact column changes owner. AI/ML systems processing personal data frequently cross the DPIA threshold; pair with `mitre-atlas.md` when they do.

**Cross-border transfers — verify current.** Transfers outside the EU/EEA require a Chapter V mechanism: an adequacy decision, Standard Contractual Clauses (with transfer impact assessment post-*Schrems II*), Binding Corporate Rules, or a derogation. This is the most volatile part of the regime — adequacy decisions (including the EU–US Data Privacy Framework) get adopted, challenged, and invalidated, and SCC versions change. Verify the current status of any transfer mechanism at use time and date the claim; do not advise from memory.

**Scoping honesty.** GDPR is extraterritorial (Art. 3): it reaches non-EU organizations offering goods or services to, or monitoring, people in the EU. Fines scale to the higher of €20M or 4% of global annual turnover for the top tier (Art. 83(5)) — which is why it gets board attention that other regimes don't. And none of this section is legal advice: the architect designs the measures; counsel and the DPO determine lawfulness, basis, and notification obligations. Say so in deliverables.

## DoD CMMC — the architect's view

Governed by the DoD CMMC program (https://dodcio.defense.gov/cmmc/); codified in 32 CFR Part 170 with contract clauses in 48 CFR (DFARS 252.204-7012/-7019/-7020/-7021). **Verify program status at use time and date every program-status claim**: the model's level count, practice counts, and assessment requirements have changed across program revisions (CMMC 1.0's five levels became 2.0's three), and the phased rollout of CMMC requirements into DoD contracts is in progress — which contracts require which level, and when, is a moving target.

**The model, as of CMMC 2.0 (verify current):** three levels distinguished by what they protect and who checks:
- **Level 1** — basic safeguarding of **FCI** (Federal Contract Information): the 15 requirements of FAR 52.204-21; annual *self*-assessment.
- **Level 2** — protection of **CUI** (Controlled Unclassified Information): the 110 requirements of **NIST SP 800-171** (Rev. 2 is the assessment baseline at the 2.0 program launch; the program's adoption timeline for Rev. 3 is exactly the kind of claim to verify and date); triennial **C3PAO** certification assessment for most CUI-handling contractors, self-assessment for a limited subset the DoD designates.
- **Level 3** — protection of CUI against APT-class threats: Level 2 plus a selected subset of **NIST SP 800-172** enhanced requirements; assessed by the government (DIBCAC).

**The load-bearing relationship: CMMC is an assessment program layered on 800-171, not a new control catalog.** The requirements at Level 2 *are* SP 800-171; Level 3 adds 800-172 enhancements. This file does not restate the 800-171 control families — see `nist-sp.md` for 800-171/800-171A/800-172 and their alignment to 800-53. Architecture work for CMMC is therefore 800-171 implementation work plus two things the SP itself doesn't give you: scoping and assessment mechanics.

**Scoping is the architecture problem.** The assessed environment is defined by where CUI is processed, stored, or transmitted, plus the assets that secure or could affect it — the CMMC scoping guidance categorizes assets (CUI assets, security protection assets, contractor risk-managed assets, specialized assets, out-of-scope) and the categorization drives assessment depth. The architect's highest-value move mirrors PCI scoping: **shrink the CUI boundary before hardening it.** The common cost-control pattern is the **enclave** — a segmented environment (dedicated network segment, VDI, or a government-cloud tenancy such as a FedRAMP-authorized/GCC-class environment for CUI workloads) that concentrates CUI handling so the certification boundary excludes the general corporate estate. An enclave is only as good as its isolation: data-flow discipline (how CUI enters and leaves), identity separation, and endpoint scoping decide whether the boundary survives assessment. Flow-down matters too — primes must ensure subcontractors handling CUI meet the required level, so the architecture question extends into the supply chain.

**Assessment ecosystem and its mechanics.** Self-assessments (Level 1, and Level 2 where permitted) are entered annually with an affirmation by a senior official — which creates False Claims Act exposure for overstated scores; certification assessments are performed by **C3PAOs** accredited through the CMMC ecosystem's accreditation body. **POA&Ms are allowed but bounded** (verify current rules): only a subset of requirements may be open, a minimum score applies, the highest-weighted requirements cannot be POA&M'd, and closure is required within a fixed window (180 days at 2.0 launch) — so "we'll POA&M it" is not a compliance strategy, it's a short-term bridge on the lower-weighted items only.

**SPRS score reality.** Under DFARS 252.204-7019/-7020, contractors handling CUI already submit a NIST SP 800-171 DoD Assessment Methodology score to **SPRS** (Supplier Performance Risk System): 110 points maximum, with weighted deductions per unimplemented requirement (some cost 5 points, so the floor is well below zero at −203). Contracting officers can see it, primes ask subs for it, and it predates CMMC enforcement — for many defense contractors the SPRS score is the *current* compliance pressure while CMMC phases in. Architect toward an honest score with a credible POA&M/SSP behind it: the System Security Plan and POA&M are the assessment artifacts, and a score that doesn't survive a DIBCAC or C3PAO look-through is a liability, not an asset.

## Crosswalk pattern

Build one control inventory, then map. High-leverage overlaps (verify exact IDs for the versions in play):

| Control capability | SOX ITGC | SOC 2 | PCI DSS | GDPR | CMMC (800-171 Rev. 2 families) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Access provisioning + reviews | Access to programs/data | CC6.1–CC6.3 | Req. 7, 8 | Art. 25/32 (access limitation) | 3.1 Access Control |
| Change management | Change management | CC8.1 | Req. 6 | Art. 25 (by design) | 3.4 Configuration Mgmt |
| Logging + monitoring | Operations | CC7.2–CC7.3 | Req. 10 | Art. 32(1)(d), 33 (detection) | 3.3 Audit & Accountability |
| Segmentation / boundary | (scope reduction) | CC6.6 | Req. 1 + scoping | Art. 32 (appropriate measures) | 3.13 System & Comms Protection + CUI scoping |
| Encryption + key mgmt | (data integrity) | CC6.1/CC6.7 | Req. 3, 4 | Art. 32(1)(a) | 3.13.11/3.13.16 |
| Vendor management | (SOC 1 reliance) | CC9.2 | Req. 12.8–12.9 | Art. 28 (processors) | flow-down to subs |
| Incident response | Operations | CC7.3–CC7.5 | Req. 12.10 | Art. 33/34 (72h) | 3.6 Incident Response |
| Backup / recovery | Operations | (Availability cat.) | Req. 12.10.1 | Art. 32(1)(c) | 3.8.9 |

The differences that break naive crosswalks: PCI is prescriptive down to parameter values where the others are objective-based; SOX cares only about financial-reporting integrity, so availability controls may be out of its scope entirely; SOC 2 periods mean evidence must exist *continuously*, not at audit time; GDPR's risk is borne by the data subject, so "appropriate to the risk" is measured against harm to individuals, not to the business; CMMC/800-171 is binary per requirement at assessment (implemented or not) with SPRS weighting, so partial implementations that would pass an objective-based regime score as zero. Note per-regime deltas in the mapping rather than claiming full equivalence.

## Boundary discipline

Gap analyses from this skill inform preparation; they are not attestations or legal advice. Only a QSA/ISA (PCI), a licensed CPA firm (SOC 2), the external financial auditor (SOX), a C3PAO/DIBCAC (CMMC certification levels), or a supervisory authority and the courts (GDPR) determines compliance. Where applicability is contestable — PCI scoping edge cases, SOX materiality, SOC 2 carve-outs, CUI identification and CMMC level applicability, GDPR lawful basis and transfer mechanisms — frame it as a position to take *to* the assessor (or counsel/DPO, for GDPR) with supporting rationale, not a settled conclusion.
