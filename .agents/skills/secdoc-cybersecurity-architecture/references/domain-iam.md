# Identity & Access Management (IAM) Architecture

## Scope

This domain covers identity architecture end to end: directory and IdP design, authentication, authorization models, privileged access, identity governance, non-human identity, and conditional access. Boundary with neighbors: network enforcement points that consume identity decisions are `domain-network-perimeter.md`; endpoint device-trust signals feed in from `domain-endpoint.md`; cloud IAM platform mechanics from `domain-cloud-hybrid.md`; SP 800-63 and 800-207 citations live in `nist-sp.md`. This file owns the identity control plane those domains depend on.

## Architecture patterns

**Identity as the control plane — the claim, argued.** The perimeter multiplied (SaaS, cloud, remote work), and the one control point present in every access path — regardless of network, device, or location — is the identity decision. Three consequences follow architecturally: (1) the IdP is tier-0 infrastructure and gets availability, change control, and monitoring to match — an IdP compromise is game over in a way no single firewall compromise is, and it is where serious intrusions now concentrate (token theft, federation abuse, consent phishing, MFA fatigue); (2) zero trust is operationally an identity program — SP 800-207's policy decision point consumes identity, device, and context signals, and the PDP most organizations deploy *is* the IdP's policy engine (`nist-sp.md`); (3) the corollary of centralization is concentration risk — the defense-in-depth caution in `architecture-best-practices.md` (three controls depending on one IdP are one layer) applies to the IdP itself: protect the recovery paths, monitor the IdP with independent telemetry, and rehearse IdP-compromise response (`domain-secops.md`).

**Directory and identity-provider architecture.** The standard enterprise pattern is hybrid: an on-prem directory (AD) synchronized to a cloud IdP, with the sync/federation tier as the load-bearing seam — protect it like the tier-0 asset it is (compromise of the sync server or federation signing keys converts on-prem compromise into cloud tenant compromise; the hybrid-boundary discussion in `domain-cloud-hybrid.md` is the same finding from the other side). Design decisions: authentication topology (cloud-side authentication with password hash sync vs. pass-through vs. federation — fewer on-prem dependencies in the auth path means fewer outage and attack surfaces), a single authoritative source per identity attribute (usually HR for humans — the joiner-mover-leaver trigger, below), and consolidation as the goal state: every directory that isn't the authoritative one is either a sync target or a migration candidate.

**Federation protocols at working depth.** **SAML 2.0** — XML assertions, the enterprise SSO incumbent; signing-certificate lifecycle and assertion-validation strictness are its recurring failure points (signature-wrapping-class flaws live in sloppy validation). **OIDC** (on OAuth 2.0) — token-based, the modern default for web/mobile/API; the architecture discipline is in the details: authorization-code flow with PKCE as the required pattern for public clients, token lifetimes and refresh-token rotation as explicit policy, audience and issuer validation enforced. **SCIM** — the provisioning protocol; SSO without SCIM (or an equivalent provisioning path) means accounts create via SSO but never deprovision — the orphaned-SaaS-account factory. Procurement rule with teeth: SSO *and* SCIM support as vendor requirements, and the SSO-tax line items surfaced in vendor review (`domain-governance.md`).

**Authentication architecture.** **SP 800-63B** is the citation anchor (verify current revision — the -4 update revised the suite): assurance levels (AAL1–3) selected per system risk, and its modern guidance internalized — password length over composition rules, no forced periodic rotation without evidence of compromise, breached-password screening. The design bar is **phishing-resistant MFA**: authenticators that cryptographically bind to the origin (FIDO2/WebAuthn passkeys, PIV/smart card) defeat the proxy-phishing kits that harvest OTP and push approvals as easily as passwords; push-with-number-matching is the floor for what remains, SMS is a documented-exception-only channel, and phishing-resistant is *required* (not preferred) for privileged and tier-0 access. **Passwordless, honestly staged**: passkeys are production-ready for the workforce web/mobile mainstream; the long tail — legacy protocol clients, shared workstations, vendor apps without WebAuthn — keeps passwords alive in pockets for years, so plan coexistence and, critically, close the back doors: **recovery and helpdesk reset flows must meet the same assurance bar as the front door** (identity-verification procedure, no reset-to-weaker-factor), because attackers already route around strong MFA through the helpdesk.

**Authorization models — with the governance cost of each.** **RBAC** — roles bundle permissions; cheap to reason about, and it decays: role explosion (a role per snowflake request) and privilege accretion (nobody removes) are its lifecycle diseases; budget periodic role rationalization or inherit a thousand-role estate. **ABAC** — policy over attributes (user, resource, environment); expressive and centralizable, but the policy engine is only as good as attribute *quality* — garbage attributes make confidently wrong decisions, so attribute governance (sources, freshness, ownership) is the hidden cost. **ReBAC** — authorization from relationship graphs ("owner of," "member of team that owns"); the natural model for collaboration platforms and fine-grained resource sharing (the Zanzibar-descended engine category), at the cost of operating a graph service in the critical path. Real estates layer them: RBAC for coarse entitlement bundles, ABAC/conditional policy for context, ReBAC where the product's sharing model demands it. Recommend by governance capacity, not expressiveness — the most powerful model an org can't maintain is worse than a simpler one it can.

**Privileged access architecture.** Standing privilege is the target; the pattern set:
- **PAM vaulting and rotation** — privileged credentials vaulted, checked out with approval and justification, rotated on return; shared/break-glass accounts under dual control with sealed-envelope procedures tested.
- **Just-in-time elevation** — the architectural goal: zero standing privilege, with time-boxed role activation (approval-gated for the top tier) replacing permanent membership; measure the estate by *standing* privileged accounts trending toward zero.
- **Session brokering** — privileged sessions pass through a broker (recording, command audit, credential injection so the admin never sees the secret) — the control that makes privileged activity investigable (`domain-secops.md` consumes the recordings) and composes with the PAW/jump-host tiering in `domain-endpoint.md`.
- Scope honesty: "privileged" includes infrastructure admins, but also SaaS tenant admins, CI/CD service accounts, and the IdP's own administrators — the PAM program that covers domain admins and ignores the identity-platform admins vaulted the wrong tier.

**Identity governance.** The process layer that breaks everywhere:
- **Joiner-mover-leaver** — HR-driven, automated end to end. Joiner: birthright access via role/attribute assignment, nothing hand-granted. **Mover is the one that fails**: transfers accumulate old access because nothing revokes it — architect movers as *re-provisioning* (old access drops except what the new role grants, with a grace overlap) rather than addition. Leaver: revocation within hours across *all* systems — which is only possible if provisioning was centralized (SCIM, above); the orphaned-account count is the metric that tells the truth.
- **Access reviews that mean something** — reviews scoped to high-risk entitlements with decision-support context (last-used data, peer comparison, risk flags) beat quarterly certify-everything campaigns that train managers to bulk-approve; measure revocation rate — a review cycle with ~0% revocations is rubber-stamping, and auditors (SOX ITGC, SOC 2 CC6.2/CC6.3) increasingly know it.
- **SoD** — toxic-combination rules defined with the business (requestor/approver, dev/prod-deploy, payment-create/payment-release), enforced preventively at provisioning where possible, detectively via analytics where not; SOX's ITGC anchor (`compliance.md`).

**Non-human identity — the growth problem.** NHIs (service accounts, workload identities, API keys, tokens, automation and AI-agent credentials) outnumber humans several-fold in most estates and get none of the lifecycle discipline: no owner, no expiry, no MFA-equivalent, passwords set in 2016. The architecture: inventory with named human owners per NHI; platform-issued workload identity replacing static credentials wherever the platform supports it (`domain-cloud-hybrid.md`); vault-and-rotate for the static remainder (`domain-appsec.md`, secrets); least-privilege scoping reviewed like human access; lifecycle tied to the owning application's lifecycle (app decommission triggers credential decommission); and monitoring tuned to NHI behavior — an interactive login by a service account is a high-fidelity detection (`domain-secops.md`).

**Conditional access as policy architecture.** The conditional-access layer is where zero trust becomes enforceable policy: decisions from user risk, device compliance (`domain-endpoint.md` signals), location/network, application sensitivity, and session context — evaluated per access, SP 800-207-style. Treat the policy set as code: named policies with owners and intent statements, a deny-by-default posture for unmanaged scenarios, report-only staging before enforcement, break-glass accounts excluded and separately monitored, and periodic gap analysis (which app/user/scenario combinations does no policy cover?). Policy sprawl is real — fifty overlapping policies nobody can reason about is its own outage-and-bypass risk; rationalize on a schedule.

## Control mapping

- **SP 800-53 Rev. 5**: AC-2 (+enhancements — account lifecycle), AC-3/AC-6 (enforcement, least privilege; AC-6(9) auditing privileged functions), IA-2 (+(1)/(2) MFA), IA-5 (authenticator management), IA-8 (non-organizational), AC-2(3) (disable inactive), AU-6 with identity telemetry, CM-5 (access restrictions for change — SoD's control home).
- **SP 800-63-4 suite**: 63A (IAL — proofing, the helpdesk-reset anchor), 63B (AAL — the phishing-resistance argument), 63C (FAL — federation assurance); **800-207** for the PDP/PEP framing (`nist-sp.md`).
- **CSF 2.0**: Protect (PR.AA — identity management, authentication, and access control as its own category), Govern for the program layer.
- **ATT&CK**: T1078 (Valid Accounts — the technique the whole domain reduces) and .004 (Cloud Accounts), T1556 (Modify Authentication Process), T1621 (MFA Request Generation — fatigue attacks), T1134/T1550 (token abuse); mitigations M1032 (MFA), M1026 (Privileged Account Management), M1015 (Active Directory Configuration); map per `mitre-attack.md`.
- **Compliance hooks**: PCI DSS Req. 7 (need-to-know) and Req. 8 (identification and authentication — 8.3.x MFA into the CDE); SOX access ITGC and SoD; SOC 2 CC6.1–CC6.3; GDPR Art. 25/32 access limitation; CMMC/800-171 3.1 (Access Control) and 3.5 (Identification and Authentication — MFA at 3.5.3) — see `compliance.md`.

## Decision criteria

- **Small org**: one cloud IdP, SSO+SCIM to every SaaS that supports it, passkeys/phishing-resistant MFA org-wide (small estates can jump straight to the good pattern), separate admin accounts, built-in JIT elevation — no standalone IGA/PAM suites yet.
- **Mid-size**: hybrid identity hardened at the sync/federation tier, conditional access as the policy backbone, PAM vaulting plus JIT for infrastructure and SaaS-admin tiers, HR-driven JML automation, NHI inventory started before it's unmapable.
- **Large / regulated**: full IGA (certification campaigns with decision support, SoD rule engine), zero-standing-privilege program with session brokering, ABAC/policy-engine investment where attribute governance can sustain it, identity threat detection wired into `domain-secops.md`, and formal identity-platform resilience (IdP outage and compromise runbooks, tested).
- Universal ordering: consolidate identity → strengthen authentication (phishing-resistant, recovery paths included) → automate lifecycle → then governance analytics and model sophistication. Buying IGA before JML automation works is buying reports about a mess.

## Assessment questions

- How many identity stores hold live credentials, which one is authoritative, and what's the consolidation plan for the rest?
- What fraction of the workforce — and what fraction of *privileged* access — uses phishing-resistant MFA? What can still authenticate with password-plus-OTP or SMS, and why?
- Walk me through a helpdesk password/MFA reset: what identity verification happens, and could a caller with LinkedIn-level knowledge pass it?
- A user transfers departments today: what access do they hold in 90 days — new role's, or both? Show a real mover from last quarter.
- How many accounts have standing privileged access, what's the trend, and what did the last access review actually revoke?
- How many non-human identities exist, how many have a named owner, and when did the oldest service-account credential last rotate?
- If the IdP were unavailable or compromised this morning, what's the tested plan — and would you detect the compromise from telemetry the IdP itself doesn't control?
- Which applications bypass SSO entirely, and who approved that?
